Your task is to create a curated playlist of three protest songs that address the struggles, hopes, and demands of the Civil Rights Movement. You can find a list of songs below.

You must create this as a Google Doc, and you must give Mr. Colin edit access to your document. You will add your link at the spreadsheet here, and you will also submit this in ManageBac.

For each song, you will write a ~300 word analytical annotation that explores how the creator uses language, style, and structure to express resistance, identity, or change.

If you use any material that comes from research, you must cite it appropriately using in-text citations and a works cited page.

  • Criterion A · Analysing (Summative)
  • Criterion C · Producing Text, strand iii (Summative)
What to write

Each Entry

Each annotation should be about 300 words, divided into at least 2 paragraphs. Each entry should include the following:

  1. Title & Creator
  2. Key Techniques & Style Choices Analyze at least two literary/musical techniques (e.g., metaphor, tone, rhythm, repetition, imagery, symbolism).
  3. Effect on the Audience How do these choices make the message more powerful or emotional? What does it make people feel or think? Think about the language and also the music.
  4. Your Interpretation What do you think this text is saying about protest or justice? Why did you choose it?
  5. Connection to Other Texts How does this song connect to another text, such as a book, poem or song, either one we’ve looked at in class or one you already know?

Songs

Song Options

Choose your three songs from this list. All of them come from the Civil Rights Era (1950–1974).

  • 1964Sam Cooke

    A Change Is Gonna Come

    Sam Cooke wrote this song after experiencing racial discrimination, including being denied service at a whites-only motel. Released in 1964, it quickly became connected to the Civil Rights Movement because it spoke to ongoing struggles for legal and social equality. It arrived during a moment of national attention on segregation, voting rights, and racial violence.

    Listen Lyrics
  • 1950s–60sOdetta

    Oh, Freedom

    Originally a spiritual sung by formerly enslaved people after the Civil War, “Oh, Freedom” was revived during the Civil Rights era. Odetta’s performances helped it become widely associated with mid-20th-century Black activism. It linked the earlier struggle for emancipation with the ongoing fight for civil rights.

    Traditional; popularized 1950s–60s. Recording: “Spiritual Trilogy,” 1956.

    Listen Lyrics
  • 1961Various

    Woke Up This Morning (With My Mind Stayed on Freedom)

    This song was adapted in 1961 by Freedom Rider Robert Wesby while he was jailed in Jackson, Mississippi. It became closely tied to the Freedom Rides, in which civil rights activists from the north would take buses down to southern states to organize voter-registration drives and other civil rights campaigns across the Deep South.

    Recording: Brown Chapel congregation, Selma.

    Listen Lyrics
  • 1930s–1960sVarious

    We Shall Not Be Moved

    Originally used in labor-union organizing in the early 20th century, the song was later adopted by civil-rights activists. It became connected to sit-ins, marches, and school-integration efforts throughout the 1950s and 1960s. Its chorus, and therefore title, comes from the Bible, showing its origins as a gospel song.

    Recording: Freedom Singers, March on Washington, 1963. Lyrics: Songs of the Southern Freedom Movement, song 1.

    Listen Lyrics
  • 1964Nina Simone

    Mississippi Goddam

    Nina Simone wrote this song immediately after two major acts of racial violence: the murder of Medgar Evers in Mississippi and the bombing of the 16th Street Baptist Church in Birmingham, Alabama. Released in 1964, many southern radio stations banned the song.

    Recording: Carnegie Hall, 1964.

    Listen Lyrics
  • 1970Nina Simone

    To Be Young, Gifted and Black

    Simone wrote this song in honor of playwright Lorraine Hansberry, who died at age 34 after becoming one of the most important Black writers of her time. Released in 1970, it aligned with the growing Black Pride and Black Arts movements.

    Listen Lyrics
  • 1970Gil Scott-Heron

    The Revolution Will Not Be Televised

    Recorded in 1970, this piece challenged the idea that major social change would be presented or controlled by mainstream media. It reflected critiques of political institutions, consumer culture, and racial inequality.

    Listen Lyrics
  • 1968James Brown

    Say It Loud – I’m Black and I’m Proud

    Released in 1968, just months after the assassination of Martin Luther King Jr., the song aligned with demands for racial justice and community empowerment. It became a popular song within the Black Power movement.

    Listen Lyrics
  • 1963Pete Seeger

    If You Miss Me at the Back of the Bus

    This song emerged during the Civil Rights Movement as a response to segregation on buses and other public facilities. Pete Seeger’s performances helped spread it among interracial activist groups. It symbolized the rejection of Jim Crow laws and the push toward integration during the early 1960s.

    Recording: Carnegie Hall, 1963. Lyrics: later cover version.

    Listen Lyrics
  • 1965The Staple Singers

    Freedom Highway

    Written for the 1965 Selma-to-Montgomery march, the song was directly connected to the struggle for voting rights in the South. It was performed at numerous events supporting the Voting Rights Act.

    Lyrics: Mavis Staples live version.

    Listen Lyrics

Criterion A: Analysing (out of 7)

Criterion A assesses how well you explain how each song’s lyrics and music create meaning and affect an audience. A level of 0 means the work does not reach the 1–2 descriptors.

Level i. Techniques, style and context ii. Effect on the audience iii. Interpretation, evidence and terminology iv. Connection to other texts
7Perceptive Explains how techniques work together, such as a metaphor reinforced by the melody or vocal delivery. Context sharpens the reading of the song. Notices subtle choices. Explains precise, nuanced effects of specific lyrical and musical choices. May consider how the song worked on its original audience and on listeners today. Gives an insightful interpretation of what the song says about protest or justice. Supports it with a range of well-chosen quotations, each explained thoroughly. Uses precise terminology. Perceptively compares how both texts use a shared feature or idea, including differences in purpose or effect, with evidence from both.
5–6Competent Analyses at least two techniques per song, covering lyrics and music, and explains how each creates meaning. Uses context to explain the song’s message. Consistently links specific choices to specific responses, explaining how each choice creates that feeling or thought. Gives a clear interpretation supported by relevant quotations and explanation. Uses accurate terminology. Explains a meaningful shared feature or idea, with evidence from both texts.
3–4Adequate Identifies at least two techniques with quotations, but the explanation is brief or uneven. Mentions context but doesn’t link it to meaning. Links some effects to choices, though not consistently. Some effects are general, such as “it makes people sad.” Interpretation is general, such as “it’s about equality.” Gives some examples and explanation. Uses some terminology. Names a connection and explains some similarity or difference, but with little evidence.
1–2Limited Mostly summarizes or paraphrases the lyrics. Names a technique with little or no explanation. Gives little or no context. States a feeling without linking it to any choice the creator made. Opinions are rarely supported by examples. Uses little or no terminology. Names a connection but doesn’t explain it.

Criterion C: Producing text (out of 7)

Criterion C assesses strand iii only: how well you select relevant details and examples to develop your ideas, and how accurately you cite research and follow the formatting instructions. A level of 0 means the work does not reach the 1–2 descriptor.

Level iii. Relevant details, citations and formatting
7Excellent Extensive, well-chosen quotations, musical details and historical facts. Every detail develops an idea. All research is cited accurately, with in-text citations and a works cited page. Formatting is correct throughout.
5–6Substantial Sufficient relevant details in each entry (about 300 words) to develop its ideas. Research is cited, with only minor errors. Formatting is mostly correct.
3–4Adequate Some relevant details. Some entries are thin, padded or well under length. Citations are incomplete or inconsistent. Some formatting instructions are not followed.
1–2Limited Few relevant details. Ideas are not developed. Research is not cited, or most formatting instructions are not followed.

Your task (packet page 3)

  1. Part A. Read the nine voices. On your packet, sort each number into what the music did: unity, courage, memory of slavery, commitment, or spreading the message. Some fit more than one. Pick the best fit.
  2. Part B. Compare the two versions of “Woke Up This Morning” and answer the questions.
  3. Part C. Connect it to your summative: pick one job music did, and name a song technique that would help it do that job.

Part A · Nine voices

1
In a sense the freedom songs are the soul of the movement.
Martin Luther King Jr., minister and SCLC president. Why We Can’t Wait (1964).
2
King, in his book about the Montgomery bus boycott, on the songs sung at its meetings:
these traditional songs, which brought to mind the long history of the Negro’s suffering
Martin Luther King Jr. Stride Toward Freedom (1958).
3
The freedom songs are playing a strong and vital role in our struggle.
Martin Luther King Jr., during the Albany Movement in Georgia. Quoted in the New York Times, August 20, 1962.
4
On the song “We Shall Overcome”:
One cannot describe the vitality and emotion this one song evokes across the Southland.
Wyatt Tee Walker, SCLC leader. In Guy and Candie Carawan, We Shall Overcome (1963).
5
Freedom Rider James Farmer, on what the jailed riders sang in Mississippi in 1961:
old folk songs and gospel songs to which new words had been written
James Farmer, CORE leader and Freedom Rider. Quoted in Sanford Wexler, The Civil Rights Movement (1993).
6
On singing together at mass meetings:
After the song, the differences among us would not be as great.
Bernice Johnson Reagon, SNCC Freedom Singer from Albany, Georgia. “In Our Hands,” Sing Out! (1976).
7
Reagon describes stories she heard about...
protesters being in jail, and the jailers saying, ‘shut up that singing.’
Bernice Johnson Reagon. “How to Think of Freedom Songs,” Eyes on the Prize, PBS American Experience.
8
I don’t have any sense of the civil rights movement existing without the singing
Bernice Johnson Reagon. Interview, Talk of the Nation, NPR, 2010.
9
Mississippi song leader Hollis Watkins, on how Fannie Lou Hamer used songs at meetings:
if they made certain commitments in songs, then she would hold them to that
Hollis Watkins, SNCC organizer. Oh Freedom Over Me, American RadioWorks, American Public Media.

About the language: in the 1950s and 60s, “Negro” was the common, polite term, and King uses it in voice 2. Today it is outdated. When you write, use “Black” or “African American.”

Part B · One song, two versions

The older gospel song

Traditional African American song. Recorded as early as 1936.

Woke up this mornin’ with my mind, stayed on Jesus.
Walkin’ and talkin’ with my mind, stayed on Jesus.
Hallelu, hallelu, hallelujah.

The freedom song

Adapted by Rev. Robert Wesby in a Mississippi jail during the Freedom Rides, 1961.

Woke up this morning with my mind stayed on freedom.
Walking and talking with my mind stayed on freedom.
Hallelu, hallelu, hallelujah!

Your task (packet page 4)

By the mid-1960s, the movement disagreed about how to win freedom. Some leaders wanted Black and white Americans to protest together, without violence, to end segregation. Others wanted Black Americans to build their own power and pride, and did not want to be limited by nonviolence. You will analyze their words the way you analyzed speeches in Unit 1.

  1. Read Pair 1 and Pair 2. Read each leader’s description too: it is part of the context.
  2. Use the four analysis tools below to complete the table on your packet for each quote.
  3. Answer the comparison questions, then predict what a protest song from each path would sound like.
1 · AudienceWho is “we”? Who is the speaker talking to?
2 · DictionWhat is the strongest word? What does it suggest?
3 · ToneCalm, urgent, angry, proud, warning…? Look at punctuation and sentence type.
4 · DemandWhat does the speaker want, and how soon?
Pair 1 · Two of the most famous leaders of the 1960s
Martin Luther King Jr.
1929–1968 · Baptist minister · President, Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC)
“As a theologian, Martin Luther King reflected often on his understanding of nonviolence.”
“Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere.”
“Letter from Birmingham Jail,” 1963, written while King was in jail for leading a protest.
Malcolm X
1925–1965 · Minister, Nation of Islam · Founder, Organization of Afro-American Unity (1964)
“the nation’s most visible proponent of Black Nationalism”
“We want freedom now, but we’re not going to get it saying ‘We Shall Overcome.’”
Speech, printed in Malcolm X Speaks (1965). “We Shall Overcome” was the best-known freedom song of the movement.
Pair 2 · Two chairmen of the same student organization, SNCC, three years apart
John Lewis
1940–2020 · Chairman, Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC), 1963–1966
“one of the civil rights movement’s most courageous young leaders”
“We march today for jobs and freedom, but we have nothing to be proud of.”
Address at the March on Washington, August 28, 1963.
Stokely Carmichael
1941–1998 · Chairman, SNCC, 1966–1967
As SNCC’s chairman, he “challenged the philosophy of nonviolence and interracial alliances”
“We want Black Power!”
Rally in Greenwood, Mississippi, June 16, 1966.
